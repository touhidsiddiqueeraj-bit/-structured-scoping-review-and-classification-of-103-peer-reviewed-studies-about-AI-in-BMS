\begin{abstract}
Machine learning (ML) has moved from a promising addition to battery research to a load-bearing component of battery management systems (BMS), but the validation practices behind the accuracy claims are rarely examined. A structured scoping review with a two-tier evidence base is presented: an \textit{eligible corpus} of 799 peer-reviewed journal articles admitted by uniform rule-based criteria from a capped 30-query Crossref retrieval (798 dated 2020--2026 plus one early-access record stamped 2027), and an in-depth \textit{classified subset} of 103 studies --- 98 from that pool plus five individually verified seed studies, one dated 2019 --- coded by function, method family, chemistry, data source, and validation practice. Union-share bibliometrics across the eligible corpus locate the center of gravity of the screened literature in health estimation (SOH), state estimation (SOC), and prognostics (RUL) --- query-tag shares, read for ordering rather than exact rates, with their union share reported in Section III-B --- with charging, thermal management, balancing, and vehicle- or fleet-level work relatively under-served for a transportation-electrification readership. Within the classified subset, three weaknesses recur. Only 8 of 88 primary-application papers evaluate online or on real vehicles; 17 of 88 rely on within-cell or k-fold splits that can leak trajectory information; and the largest single data source is private laboratory cells (36 of 88), a reproducibility constraint at least as limiting as the benchmark concentration (23 papers on three datasets). Validation practice shows no detectable difference between physics-informed/hybrid and purely data-driven papers under either definition of stringency --- an underpowered contrast in which meaningful differences in either direction cannot be excluded --- and symmetric and strong-to-weak label-noise scenarios bracket the strong-protocol share between 11\% and 59\%, the strong-to-weak floor a worst-case bound rather than an estimate. A post-hoc recall audit against the included reviews' reference lists bounds what the capped retrieval misses. The paper closes with a requirement-level mapping of BMS functions onto UN GTR 20, GB 38031, and ISO 26262, a positioning against prior reviews, and a twelve-item reporting checklist; all data and code are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The battery management problem}

Lithium-ion batteries now anchor three converging transitions --- electrified road transport, renewable-heavy stationary storage, and the electrification of aerospace and marine systems --- and in each the battery is an actively managed asset whose usable capacity, lifetime, and safety depend on real-time operating decisions. The battery management system (BMS) is the embedded cyber-physical layer that makes those decisions --- sensing, state estimation, protection, balancing, and charging negotiation \cite{r1}. The difficulty stems from the fact that the quantities that matter most --- state of charge (SOC), state of health (SOH), internal temperature, and the onset of degradation or fault mechanisms --- are not directly measurable and must be inferred from noisy, partially observable terminal measurements under widely varying conditions. Model-based approaches built on equivalent-circuit or electrochemical models face a persistent fidelity-versus-tractability trade-off, and their parameters drift as cells age \cite{r2}. It is in this inference-and-decision layer that machine learning has made its most forceful entry, and the stakes are highest in transportation electrification: electric-vehicle packs face automotive safety expectations --- UN GTR 20 on board energy storage rechargeable systems, GB 38031 on traction battery safety requirements, and ISO 26262 on functional safety of electric/electronic systems --- that make state estimation, degradation prognosis, and fault detection safety-relevant BMS functions, not research add-ons. Section IX returns to these three standards and maps each estimation function to the property the standard actually demands (detection reliability, gradient/temperature limits, protection timing) --- the mapping that turns the validation findings of this review into vehicle-level stakes.

Machine learning entered battery management through estimation on hand-engineered features. Two studies then reset expectations. Severson and colleagues predicted the entire cycle life of lithium-ion cells with a median test error near 9\% from the first hundred cycles alone, on a public dataset of 124 cells \cite{r3}. Attia and colleagues demonstrated closed-loop Bayesian optimization of fast-charging protocols, identifying high-cycle-life protocols among 224 candidates in 16 days of cycling rather than the more than 500 days an exhaustive search would take \cite{r4}. The five years that followed produced applications across every BMS function: recurrent and convolutional networks for SOC and SOH \cite{r5}\cite{r6}, graph and transformer architectures for prognostics \cite{r7}\cite{r8}, physics-informed neural networks embedding electrochemical and thermal priors \cite{r9}\cite{r10}, transfer learning across chemistries and vehicles \cite{r11}\cite{r12}, reinforcement learning agents \cite{r13}, and, most recently, LLM-based studies of station-side incident-risk analysis and vehicle-cloud management \cite{r14}\cite{r15}.

\subsection{Existing reviews and the literature gap}

The review literature has grown alongside the applications but is fragmented along the same axes as the primary studies: an early perspective on data-driven SOC and SOH prediction \cite{r16} set the agenda; function-specific reviews cover SOC estimation \cite{r17}\cite{r18}, SOH estimation \cite{r19}, and prognostics \cite{r20}; method-specific reviews follow deep-learning state estimation \cite{r1}, physics-informed impedance diagnostics \cite{r21}, or interpretable prognosis \cite{r22}; and domain reviews examine fault diagnosis \cite{r23}\cite{r24} and thermal management \cite{r25}, alongside broader degradation-method surveys \cite{r26} and EV-powertrain ML reviews with BMS scope \cite{r27}. What is missing is a cross-functional account of the field's trajectory: which method families are used for which tasks, on which data, validated how --- and, among existing cross-functional surveys, none to our knowledge quantifies validation practice at all. Table VII in Section IX positions this review against its closest competitors and states the added value explicitly; the short version is that prior reviews synthesize \textit{what is claimed}, while this review additionally measures --- within a 103-study classified subset --- how those claims are validated, ties both to three safety standards, and releases the record-level dataset to make every count auditable.

\subsection{Contribution of this paper}

This paper makes four contributions: (i) a rule-based \textit{eligible corpus} of 799 peer-reviewed journal articles (2020--2026), selected by uniform criteria applied to all screened Crossref records --- no function quotas, year caps, or citation thresholds --- so that field-level shares are measured, not curated, with the retrieval, screening, and tagging provenance of every record released; (ii) an in-depth \textit{classified subset} of 103 studies from that corpus, coded along the six classification fields (function, method family, chemistry, data source, validation practice, and coded finding/limitation summaries) with the classification dataset, codebook, per-paper protocol audit, and a 30-paper blind inter-coder reliability study released together with a label-noise sensitivity analysis of the headline counts; (iii) a two-tier synthesis --- measured bibliometrics from the eligible corpus (Section III) plus function-by-function critical review of the classified subset (Sections IV--VIII) --- with a BMS-function-to-safety-standard mapping and a gap-driven research agenda including a twelve-item reporting checklist (Section IX); and (iv) a transportation-electrification cut of the corpus (Section III-B) separating cell-level laboratory evidence from pack-, vehicle-, and fleet-level practice. Fig. 1 previews the classified subset, mapping it across management functions and method families: the center of gravity sits in the health-prognostics complex, hybrid model--data approaches span every function, and learning-based charging control remains a concentrated Bayesian-optimization stronghold. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section II details the review methodology and its limitations. Section III presents the two-tier corpus overview, five-year bibliometrics, and the transportation-electrification cut. Sections IV--VII review machine learning for state estimation, health estimation and prognostics, charging control, and thermal management, faults, and safety. Section VIII examines cross-cutting directions, and Sections IX and X give the research agenda and conclusions.

\section{Review Methodology}

\subsection{Search strategy}

All candidate records were retrieved programmatically from the Crossref REST API between 4 and 5 September 2026 using 30 bibliographic query strings spanning the application areas above. Queries were issued in five passes: a citation-sorted 2020--2026 pass, dedicated 2020--2022 and 2023 windows, a mid-window 2021--2023 coverage pass, and a relevance-sorted recency pass starting June 2024, yielding 3,000 records; after DOI-based deduplication (1,450 duplicates removed), 1,550 unique records remained. Six landmark studies --- including \cite{r3} and \cite{r2} --- were additionally safeguarded as forced seeds: five were retrieved and verified individually because citation-sorted retrieval had crowded them out, and one was already among the top-cited hits. Four properties should be stated plainly. First, the Crossref registry is a metadata registry, not a discovery engine like Scopus or Web of Science; it was chosen so that every included record is individually verifiable by direct DOI resolution, at the cost of a recall floor. Second, citation-ordered passes favor established English-language work; all trend statements therefore describe this citation-weighted, journal-article sample rather than the field in the aggregate. Third, January--May 2024 is a known retrieval hole --- the citation-sorted pass returns almost nothing from those months and the recency pass starts at June 2024 --- so the 2024 dip visible in year distributions is a design artifact of this retrieval, not evidence about publication volume; per-year counts in Section III-A are therefore reported as counts within the capped retrieval, with the hole documented, and no field-level per-year shares are claimed. Fourth, every query pass returned its top 20 records only, so the 30 queries are design-capped at 100 records per query family; a post-hoc recall audit against the deposited reference lists of the 15 included reviews finds that the retrieval contains 112 of the 649 in-scope-looking, 2020-onward, DOI-bearing references they cite (17.3\% counted per-source; 96 of 586 unique references, 16.4\%). Retrieval recall is therefore bounded and now quantified: theme frequencies, per-family shares, and `earliest' claims in this review are scoped to this retrieval and the subset it fed, and the LLM-estimator recall miss disclosed in the cross-cutting section is cited by none of those reviews either (audit released as recall\_audit.py/.json with cached responses).

\subsection{Screening and rule-based selection}

The screening funnel is shown in Fig. 2. A title-level relevance gate requiring both battery-related and machine-learning terminology retained 883 records and excluded 667 (predominantly adjacent-field papers that surfaced through loose matching); the gate is a mechanical keyword rule, and its term lists are released (tagging\_terms.json) together with all 1,550 screening decisions (screening\_log.csv). Venue and document-type screening removed 84 records --- conference abstracts and proceedings series, preprint servers, and non-scholarly venues --- leaving 799 eligible records. Selection then proceeds in two tiers, replacing the single-quota design that an earlier draft of this review used. \textit{Tier 1 (eligible corpus).} Every one of the 799 eligible records is included, subject only to venue screening; no function quotas, year caps, or citation thresholds are applied. Every one of the 799 eligible records carries a Crossref-registered DOI, and every record carried forward to Tier 2 full-text coding was individually DOI-resolved. Function tagging at this tier is inherited from retrieval: each record carries the area of every query theme that returned it (query list released), so a record can hold several tags; 202 of 799 records (25\%) carry no management-function tag --- most are method-level or cross-cutting records (physics-informed, second life, digital twins, benchmark papers) --- so per-function shares below are computed over tagged records and cross-function totals are reported as union shares, never as sums of per-tag percentages. \textit{Tier 2 (classified subset).} Because in-depth coding of chemistry, data source, and validation practice requires full-text assessment, full-text coding was applied to a classified subset of 103 studies --- 98 from the eligible pool plus 5 landmark seeds retrieved and verified individually --- curated with the same eight-function stratification used by the tagging pass, with six landmark studies (including the 2019 field-defining seed, Severson et al.) safeguarded as forced entries --- five retrieved individually from outside the query pool and one already among the top-cited hits (Section II-A). This subset is curated, not sampled: the released record-level log identifies every record at every screening stage (with the forced seeds annotated), the released selection files allow every inclusion and drop to be traced, and its function and year composition does not match the eligible corpus's measured shares exactly. Section III therefore never generalizes subset distributions to the field; where the subset is the only source (method family, validation practice), the text reports subset statistics and their selection caveats explicitly.

\subsection{Classification framework, validation definitions, and limitations}

Each paper was classified along six fields: management function (eight values, including a cross-cutting cluster); dominant method family (classical ML, deep learning, physics-informed/hybrid, optimization-augmented, reinforcement/control learning, transfer learning, foundation models/LLMs, review); chemistry; primary data source; validation approach; and a coded summary of the headline finding and principal limitation. Three terms are used consistently: an \textit{eligible record} is one of the 799 Tier-1 studies; a \textit{classifiable paper} is one of the 103 Tier-2 studies; and a \textit{primary-application paper} is one of the 88 classified papers whose contribution is a primary application --- the 12 method-coded reviews and 3 review-type syntheses coded under their dominant method family are excluded from every rate that uses this denominator (Table II counts them per function; papers.csv identifies each one). Validation practice is coded against four definitions used throughout this paper: (1) \textit{within-cell split} --- train and test segments drawn from the same cycling trajectory; (2) \textit{unsupervised k-fold cross-validation} --- folds assigned over samples of the same trajectories without cell isolation; (3) \textit{cross-cell holdout} --- every test cell held out entirely from training (with cross-chemistry and cross-domain holdout extending this isolation across chemistry or application); and (4) \textit{online or real-vehicle/fleet validation} --- the deployed system evaluated on live vehicle data. Cross-cell, cross-chemistry, cross-domain, and online/fleet are together the \textit{strong protocols}; the four remaining coded environments (experimental hardware, simulation plus hardware spot-check, simulation-only, case study) characterize where a study was exercised but say nothing about train--test isolation, and they are reported separately --- never mixed into strong-protocol percentages. Borderline assignments, the absorbing rule for ambiguous cases (a study is credited with the \textit{strongest} protocol it reports for its headline claim), and the decision tree with examples are given in the released codebook. Classification was performed by the lead author with conservative coding from full texts; to probe the reliability of that coding, two co-author coders (the second and third authors, not involved in the primary coding) later blind-re-coded a 30-paper stratified random sample (drawn by function and year band, seed-pinned and reproducible) under the released codebook, without access to the released labels, to the population statistics, or to each other. The blinding was procedural rather than external: the coders are authors of this study --- their later author-contribution roles included verification of classifications and counts --- but their coding sheets were locked and hash-tracked at completion, with a filed blinding memo documenting what each coder had opened before coding. Blind agreement between the two coders is strong on three of the five classification fields: management function ($\kappa$ = 0.92, Krippendorff $\alpha$ = 0.92), method family ($\kappa$ = 0.88, $\alpha$ = 0.88), and primary data source ($\kappa$ = 0.88, $\alpha$ = 0.88). Two fields fell below the $\kappa$ $\ge$ 0.66 threshold: chemistry ($\kappa$ = 0.55) and validation protocol ($\kappa$ = 0.48). Part of the shortfall is an evidence-access artifact: the two coders had full-text (or preprint) access to only 7 and 2 of the 30 sample papers, respectively, so 23 and 28 were coded from abstracts alone --- and these two fields are precisely those least often stated at abstract level. But it must be reported straight that the aggregation Section III's counts rest on does not escape this: on strong-protocol versus non-strong, the two coders agree on 25 of 30 papers (raw agreement 83\%, $\kappa$ = 0.51 at the sample's prevalence --- below the 0.66 threshold the paper itself applies). The released labels are therefore treated as one plausible coding, and all headline validation counts in Section III carry a scenario sensitivity analysis built from the observed coder-versus-label disagreement in this sample: the central strong-protocol estimate (48\% of the 88 primary papers) spans 40--59\% under the symmetric scenario, and a strong-to-weak worst-case bound (Section III-C) places it as low as 18\% at the observed abstract-level recode rate --- so the strong-protocol level is a bounded range rather than a pinned estimate, and no qualitative conclusion below depends on where in these ranges the truth lies (Section III-C). Coder access constraints, training, and the complete per-paper, per-field disagreement table are released together with both locked coding sheets and the hash-tracked log; protocol-versioning candidates (a ``protocol not reported'' code; a chemistry inference rule) are logged as candidates and alter no released label. The synthesis proceeds on two tracks: measured bibliometrics from the eligible corpus and cross-tabulations from the classified subset, both generated programmatically from the released datasets (Section III), and qualitative function-by-function critique (Sections IV--VIII). The methodology's limitations are acknowledged: Crossref coverage excludes influential conference papers; title-level screening can miss relevant work; citation-ordered retrieval privileges established English-language venues; the per-year retrieval window has one documented hole (Section II-A); the Tier-2 subset is curated rather than sampled, so validation-practice rates are subset statistics that do not automatically generalize to the eligible corpus or the field; and reliability is documented on a 30-paper blind re-coding subset rather than measured corpus-wide (three of five fields above threshold, two below, with the resulting sensitivity ranges reported). DOI resolution, moreover, verifies that a record exists, not that its venue is topically sensible: one classified-subset study (a physics-informed SOC network, \cite{r10}) appears in an RFID-special-issue journal --- we flag it here rather than dropping it, and the released audit file (final\_audit.json) records each reference's Crossref-resolved title and venue at retrieval time so that misattributions of this kind are hidden. These are the reasons this paper describes itself as a scoping review with bibliometric classification rather than a meta-analysis.

\section{Corpus Overview and Five-Year Evolution}

\subsection{Publication trend and method-family evolution}

Two layers of trend evidence are reported. On the \textit{measured} tier (eligible corpus, 799 records), the capped retrieval captures 55, 85, 139, and 223 journal articles for 2020--2023, 56 for 2024 --- an observed count inside the capped retrieval, not an estimate of missing volume; the January--May 2024 window is only partially covered (Section II-A) --- then 126 in 2025 and --- partial year, retrieval date September 2026 --- 114 in 2026, plus one further early-access record date-stamped 2027 by Crossref metadata and omitted from the year chart (Fig. 3, left panel). These are counts within a capped retrieval, not census totals, and they do not support absolute-growth claims (``the literature quadrupled'' would require an uncapped retrieval). A per-year external denominator was tested and discarded: Crossref's reported result totals for the same 30 queries (a published script and its output ship with the deposit) rely on a loose token-level bibliographic match whose totals run into the hundreds of thousands and vary strongly with query phrasing, so they are not interpretable as field denominators. What remains defensible is direction within the sample: the eligible-corpus counts rise through 2020--2023, dip at the retrieval hole, and remain high in 2025--2026, and the tagged composition of those records shifts toward estimation and hybrid methods (next paragraph).

On the \textit{classified subset}, the compositional shift in method families (Fig. 3, right panel; Table I) is a within-subset sequence, not a measured field trend. Classical ML --- Gaussian process regression, support vector machines, ensembles, shallow networks on engineered features --- contributes 12 of the 23 subset papers published from 2019 through 2021, while physics-informed and hybrid model--data learning rises year by year (1, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, and 5 primary papers in 2020--2026) --- the only family with a monotonically non-decreasing trajectory within the subset --- so that 13 of its 22 primary papers fall in 2024--2026 and it is the largest single family in 2026 (Table II gives the subset totals by year). Optimization-augmented learning (Bayesian optimization, surrogate-assisted and meta-heuristic schemes) peaks in 2024 (5 of its 11 papers); transfer learning accelerates in 2024--2025 (3 of its 5 papers); reinforcement learning appears as the dominant family in 1 paper and as an embedded component in at least one more \cite{r13}\cite{r28}; the subset's first foundation-model and LLM-based studies appear in 2025--2026 (2 papers) \cite{r14}\cite{r15}, with journal-published LLM-based battery estimation beginning earlier still, as a retrieval miss rather than a venue exclusion \cite{r29}. These family shares describe the subset only; the subset is curated, small-denominator years carry wide sampling error, and the eligible pool's method-theme tags show no monotonic physics-informed trend. Two qualifications matter: the families are not mutually exclusive (a PSO-tuned LSTM is both deep learning and optimization-augmented), so the composition understates method mixing; and physics-informed papers are, on average, validated no more stringently than others even under the most permissive definition of stringency --- a point quantified, with its sensitivity ranges, in Section III-C.

\subsection{Measured application areas, the transportation-electrification cut, and venues}

Because function tags were applied mechanically to all 799 eligible records, the application-area distribution is \textit{measured} rather than quoted from a curated sample. Tags are inherited from the retrieving queries (a record carries every area whose query returned it); 597 records (75\%) carry at least one management-function tag, while the remaining 202 are method-, second-life-, digital-twin-, benchmark-level, or unmapped records without a specific BMS function, and the tags match the subset's full-text function labels for 53 of the 75 core-area papers found in the pool (71\%, any-tag match --- a lenient criterion, so shares are read for ordering, not for exact rates). Of all eligible records, health estimation (SOH) leads at 20\% (158 records; 27\% of those function-tagged), followed by state estimation (SOC) at 17\% (133), prognostics (RUL) at 13\% (103), thermal management at 12\% (99), fault and safety at 12\% (96), charging control at 8\% (64), and cell balancing at 5\% (38). These per-tag percentages must not be summed --- a record can carry several tags; the union of SOC, SOH, and RUL covers 46\% of eligible records, and adding the four control-and-safety functions brings management-function coverage to 75\%. The estimation concentration is a measured pattern consistent with the commercial pull of lifetime prediction for warranties and second-life markets, and is mirrored (with a heavier cross-cutting share) in the classified subset (Fig. 1; Table II gives subset composition by year; Table IV maps representative studies). Two measured concentrations matter specifically for transportation electrification. First, the electrification cut: only $\approx$2\% of eligible records (n = 17) are tagged fleet- or cloud-orchestration-adjacent, and in the classified subset only 10 of 88 primary papers use real vehicle or fleet data as their primary source against 36 resting on laboratory cells --- the classified literature is predominantly cell-level and laboratory-scale, one integration level below the pack- and vehicle-level questions that UN GTR 20, GB 38031, and ISO 26262 pose. Second, venue concentration is marked: in the eligible corpus the Journal of Energy Storage (103 records), Energy (61), and Batteries (45) lead, and the same two energy-storage journals head the classified subset (Journal of Energy Storage with 15 papers and Energy with 13, Applied Energy third at 9) --- energy-storage venues, not transportation-electrification venues, absorb the field's output. The thin representation of cell balancing is also telling: balancing remains dominated by classical control practice, and the subset's most-cited balancing-related result is the demonstration that routine balancing can be dispensed with in well-managed packs, reducing hazardous lithium-plating side reactions \cite{r30}.

\subsection{Data landscape and validation practice}

The data underpinning the classified subset are strikingly concentrated: 36 of the 88 primary-application papers rest on laboratory cycling of the authors' own cells; the public Severson/Toyota--MIT--Stanford (MATR) dataset alone supports 11, NASA and CALCE 6 each, and laboratory impedance spectroscopy 6; 12 are simulation-based. Only 10 papers use real vehicle or fleet data as their primary source, and \textit{data use} is a distinct measure from \textit{validation protocol}: 8 of the 10 fleet-data papers are validated online or on real vehicles, and the other two through cross-domain holdout or case-study protocols (Table III). Validation practice is arguably the classified subset's most consequential weakness, and it is now defined and audited explicitly. Per the definitions of Section II-C, cross-cell holdout --- the strongest common laboratory protocol --- accounts for 24 primary-application papers, cross-chemistry holdout for 7, cross-domain holdout for 3, and online or fleet validation for 8; together these \textit{strong protocols} cover 42 of the 88 primary-application papers (48\%). Within-cell splits (12 papers) and unsupervised k-fold cross-validation (5) leak trajectory information between train and test, producing optimistic errors that do not survive cell-to-cell variability; the remaining 29 studies establish hardware operation (20), simulation- or case-level evidence without a generalization protocol (9) --- 42+17+29 = 88. The complete per-paper protocol assignment is released in papers.csv (valid field) and tabulated as a supplement (protocol\_audit.csv), so the 42-of-88 arithmetic above can be verified row by row. The 30-paper blind re-coding of Section II-C is an abstract-level inter-coder agreement check whose sample includes the 5 review-labelled papers --- not an independent re-derivation of these assignments (their bucket agreement with the released labels is itself weak, Section II-C); the counts instead carry the scenario sensitivity that follows.

Crossing validation practice with method family, the cautious reading is: 8 of the 22 physics-informed/hybrid primary papers (36\%) use a strong protocol, against 34 of the 66 other primary papers (52\%) --- Fisher's exact p = 0.32, odds ratio 0.54 (95\% CI 0.21--1.46). Under the earlier, broader definition that also counted experimental-hardware demonstration as strong validation, the contrast is 13 of 22 (59\%) versus 49 of 66 (74\%), OR 0.50 (95\% CI 0.19--1.36, p = 0.19). We report both and claim neither as a model-family effect: the difference is not statistically significant under either definition, confounding is unadjusted (physics-informed work skews toward early-stage and simulation-heavy studies), and n = 22 is small. A minimum-detectable-effect analysis (released with the corpus) shows that 80\% power at $\alpha$ = 0.05 would require a gap of roughly 30--35 percentage points between the two families, while the observed 15-point gap is detectable with only about 20--25\% power --- so no detected difference does not mean no difference: meaningful effects in either direction remain compatible with these counts. A label-noise scenario envelope built from the observed blind coder-versus-label disagreement (Section II-C) moves the overall strong-protocol share across 35--52 of the 88 papers (40--59\% of the primary papers) under symmetric random flipping and leaves the method-family contrast non-significant in 94\% of scenario draws; the observed disagreements are directional and concentrated on strong labels (16 of 19 have the coder defaulting to weak, and each coder recoded 8 of the 13 strong-labelled sample papers weak), so a strong-to-weak scenario --- reported as a worst-case bound, because the abstract-level coders defaulted to weak wherever a protocol was not stated --- falls to about 16 papers (18\%) at the observed recode rate, with an envelope of 10--23 papers (11--26\%) and the contrast non-significant in 98\% of draws. The method-family contrast is thus robust to plausible coding error in either direction, while the strong-protocol level itself is not pinned by these counts --- the worst-case bound says how low it could sit if abstract-level recoding were taken at face value, not where it sits. The noise model is explicit and released (sensitivity\_label\_noise.py): in each of 4,000 seeded Monte Carlo draws, a strong/non-strong label-flip rate is drawn from a Normal distribution with standard deviation 0.02, truncated at zero --- centered on the coders' overall bucket error rate (0.37 and 0.27; mean 0.32) for the symmetric scenario, and on the strong-label-specific recode rate (0.62) for the strong-to-weak scenario --- and the drawn rate then flips bucket labels across the 88 primary papers before both the overall share and the Fisher test are recomputed. The honest reading remains: a physics prior does not \textit{demonstrably} buy validation rigor --- and settling the question requires the standardized protocols proposed in Section IX, not more subset cross-tabs.

The data concentration can be stated as a two-pole fragmentation metric (Fig. 4). Across 2019--2026, 23 primary papers --- 26\% of the 88 primary-application papers --- rest on the same three public benchmarks (Severson/MATR, NASA, CALCE), whose combined cell count is on the order of a few hundred; meanwhile, the single largest source overall is private laboratory cycling (36 papers) whose data are rarely released. On the shared benchmarks, dataset-specific failure modes and implicit test-set familiarity inflate apparent comparability; on the private datasets, reproducibility is unavailable in principle. Both faces of this pattern --- a small, slowly broadening empirical base under a multiplying literature, and unauditable private data beside it --- make two-pole data fragmentation the most reproducibility-relevant feature of the classified subset's data landscape; benchmark reuse itself is erratic year to year (1--7 papers) rather than tracking output, so no saturation trend is claimed.

\section{Machine Learning for Battery State Estimation}

SOC estimation is the most mature BMS function, and the classified subset shows how machine learning entered it. The early pattern pairs learning with classical estimation: Gaussian process regression for pack-level SOC with native uncertainty quantification \cite{r31}, and --- most influentially --- recurrent networks embedded in unscented Kalman filters, where the LSTM supplies a learned observation model and the filter recursive correction \cite{r5}. A second generation tuned network structure itself: particle-swarm-optimized LSTM improved accuracy and noise robustness \cite{r32}, and evolutionary-assisted CNN--BiLSTM hybrids report sub-1\% drive-cycle errors \cite{r33}. Simulation-informed training pipelines substitute automotive-accurate simulated data for exhaustive cell testing \cite{r34}, comparative benchmarks on standard drive cycles weigh ML regression families \cite{r35}, pack heterogeneity is surveyed in a joint review of pack-SOC, cell-inconsistency, and balancing estimation \cite{r36}, unified frameworks estimate SOH and SOC jointly from partial charging data \cite{r37}, large-format applications extend estimation to containerized storage strings \cite{r38}, and a review of cutting-edge SOC algorithms weighs accuracy against embedded compute budgets \cite{r39} alongside primary comparative benchmarks \cite{r40}.

The most substantive recent shift is toward hybrid schemes in which a filter, an equivalent-circuit model, or a physics prior constrains the learner: GA-optimized LSTM coupled with improved adaptive Kalman filtering reaches low error under measurement noise that breaks pure-network estimators \cite{r41}; equivalent-circuit-informed ML on impedance features estimates SOC across temperatures \cite{r42}; and physics-informed recurrent structures embed battery dynamics directly into the training objective \cite{r10}. These schemes trade engineering complexity for extrapolation behavior --- the property that matters most onboard. Yet 11 of the 13 subset SOC primary papers rest on the authors' own laboratory cells and one on laboratory impedance measurements, 9 of the 13 on a single cathode chemistry (NMC), and the thirteenth --- the cluster's only operation-scale demonstration, a simulated stationary containerized storage system rather than a vehicle --- is \cite{r38}; benchmark heterogeneity makes cross-paper comparison nearly meaningless \cite{r1}\cite{r18}. Within the subset's own reporting, accuracy on laboratory cells no longer appears to be the binding constraint in this cluster; guaranteed accuracy on arbitrary packs over full cell aging, which the onboard estimator would need, is not demonstrated anywhere in the subset.

\section{Machine Learning for Health Estimation and Prognostics}

SOH estimation and RUL prediction form the largest functional block of the classified subset (32 primary papers: 19 SOH, 13 RUL). The founding pattern is classical: extract health-sensitive features from partial charge or discharge curves, then regress with Gaussian process or similar shallow models --- incremental-capacity analysis with explicit uncertainty bounds \cite{r43}, differential thermal voltammetry \cite{r44}, and Gaussian-process classification of impedance spectra that identifies degradation modes \cite{r45}, and deep Gaussian processes coupling prognosis with degradation-mode identification \cite{r46}. Measurement relaxations followed: random partial charging data proved sufficient for accurate SOH \cite{r47}, and charging-segment adjustment refined features for vehicle-level use \cite{r48}. The landmark systematization is Roman et al.'s 179-cell pipeline showing that relaxed charge-curve features support accurate SOH estimation across a heterogeneous commercial fleet \cite{r49}.

Deep architectures entered as sequence models over capacity-fade trajectories and representation learners over raw curves: graph convolutional networks fusing multiple health indicators \cite{r7}, graph networks over partial discharge curves \cite{r50}, BiLSTM--Transformer hybrids under fast-charging stress \cite{r8}, hybrid-fusion networks for real-time prediction streams \cite{r51}, and interpretable deep learning with transparency mechanisms \cite{r52}. Field-data studies absorb real-vehicle noise \cite{r6} and estimate pack-level SOH from fleet streams \cite{r53}, while deep models estimate SOH without additional degradation experiments by learning trajectory features that generalize across chemistries \cite{r54}. Early-life prediction --- forecasting lifetime from the first cycles --- compresses test times from months to days \cite{r3}\cite{r55}\cite{r56}, hybrid physics-plus-learned models give early RUL from limited cycles \cite{r57}, the approach extends to lithium-metal chemistries \cite{r58}, and it now runs on embedded boards in real time \cite{r59}.

The prognostics cluster shows the clearest physics-informed turn: physics-informed online degradation models \cite{r60}, hybrid reinforcement learning that reduces the data demand of SOH estimation \cite{r28}, a generalizable PINN from limited early-cycle data \cite{r61}, EIS-informed physics-constrained networks \cite{r62}, a generic physics-informed RUL framework for small early-life samples \cite{r63}, multi-feature hybrids across datasets \cite{r64}, recurrent networks anticipating uncertain duty \cite{r65}, and signal-decomposition hybrids on noisy capacity series \cite{r66}. Transfer learning attacks generalization directly: domain-adversarial training across operating conditions \cite{r11}, deep transfer with partial charging curves across cell types \cite{r12}, transfer under fast-charging protocols \cite{r67}, cross-chemistry EIS estimation \cite{r68}, and a general framework quantifying the laboratory-to-fleet domain gap \cite{r69}. The critical assessment: the cluster's accuracy claims are established overwhelmingly on public benchmark cells --- 10 of the 32 primary studies in this complex use within-cell or k-fold validation (17 of the 88 primary papers in total) --- and the transfer-learning papers, the honest exception, reveal how much shift remains unaddressed. SOH estimation under controlled conditions shows signs of approaching incremental returns within this subset; calibrated lifetime and safety margins for arbitrary packs in the field remain open \cite{r2}\cite{r20}.

\section{Machine Learning for Charging Control and Optimization}

Charging control is the function where machine learning has changed experimental practice most directly. The defining result is Attia et al.: a Bayesian optimization loop identified high-cycle-life protocols among 224 candidates in 16 days of closed-loop cycling, against more than 500 days for an exhaustive search without early prediction \cite{r4}. Ensemble surrogates make multi-objective strategy optimization tractable \cite{r71}, and Bayesian fast-charging optimization reduces charge time under degradation constraints \cite{r78}. A second line embeds degradation mechanisms into the decision itself: machine-learning fast charging that perceives and regulates internal lithium-plating onset enables plating-free rapid protocols \cite{r70}, adaptive multistage schemes adjust stage boundaries against measured degradation \cite{r79}, module-level optimization manages temperature gradients \cite{r80}, and pack-level extensions coordinate balance-aware charging \cite{r81}\cite{r82}.

The deployment gap is most concrete here: of the eight charging papers, four demonstrate on laboratory hardware --- including the 224-cell closed-loop campaign above --- and the remaining four rest on simulation-level evidence (three simulation-only, one with hardware spot-checks). No subset charging paper demonstrates a learned strategy validated online on a vehicle. The unsolved problem is certifiable learning-based charging --- which is why mechanism-aware schemes such as plating-perception control \cite{r70} are the cluster's most deployment-relevant results.

\section{Machine Learning for Thermal Management, Faults and Safety}

Thermal management (7 primary papers) divides by purpose. For estimation, ANN models predict cell temperature across operating conditions \cite{r72} and internal-temperature sensing extends to ultrahigh-rate discharge where surface measurement fails \cite{r83}. For design, ML surrogates amortize expensive CFD evaluation across optimization loops \cite{r84}\cite{r73}, with experimentally validated parallel-liquid-cooling designs closing the loop \cite{r85}. For control, ANN controllers demonstrate parasitic-load reductions in simulation \cite{r86} --- but no subset paper closes this loop on hardware, leaving thermal control where charging control was five years ago. Physics-informed heat-generation estimation embeds thermal priors into the learning problem \cite{r9}, and the cluster is consolidated by a state-of-the-art review \cite{r25}.

Fault and safety applications (7 primary papers) divide into diagnosis of realized faults and prediction of impending hazards: grid-search-optimized SVMs classify sensor and connection faults \cite{r74}, curvilinear-distance methods detect multiple pack fault types in operating fleets \cite{r87}, and multi-scenario deep anomaly detection covers real EV operating envelopes \cite{r88}; force-signal deep learning catches lithium-plating-type defects at manufacturing \cite{r89}. The hazard-prediction side is increasingly model-based: multiphysics models coupled with ML predict module-level thermal-runaway progression \cite{r75}, a multiphysics-informed DeepONet augments scarce experimental events with virtual data \cite{r90}, and multi-source domain transfer moves thermal-runaway diagnosis across fleets with small target samples \cite{r76}. The reviews in this cluster are candid that laboratory fault diagnosis has historically failed to transfer to real vehicles \cite{r24}\cite{r23}; the fleet-data anomaly-detection studies are therefore the cluster's most valuable trend, even though label scarcity for rare faults keeps evaluation largely qualitative.

\section{Cross-Cutting Directions}

\subsection{Physics-informed learning, interpretability, and uncertainty}

Physics-informed and hybrid model--data learning is the classified subset's fastest-growing methodological theme (22 primary-application papers across all functions; Table I) --- in state estimation \cite{r10}\cite{r91}, heat-generation estimation \cite{r9}, prognostics \cite{r60}, and impedance-based diagnostics --- consolidated by a physics-informed EIS-diagnostics review \cite{r21} --- with physics-constrained autoencoders improving health prediction over purely learned counterparts \cite{r92} and a reviewed physical-plus-data-driven fusion literature for internal-state estimation \cite{r93}. The caution is that most physics-informed studies embed simplified physics --- equivalent-circuit or reduced electrochemical models --- so the hybrid inherits model bias even as it gains structure, and its validation is no stronger than that of purely data-driven peers (Section III-C). A smaller but consequential cluster makes learning systems inspectable: explainability-driven feature selection improves estimator robustness \cite{r94}, interpretable ML links manufacturing coating parameters to cell capacity \cite{r95}, interpretable hybrid ML untangles degradation chemistries in lithium--sulfur cells \cite{r96}, and uncertainty-aware models deliver calibrated early-life predictions \cite{r77}. For safety cases only calibrated uncertainty has a route into certification; attribution supports debugging.

\subsection{Digital twins, cloud BMS, and new sensing}

System-level architecture is emerging as a research object: digital twins track individual battery aging through incremental learning \cite{r97} and couple LSTM surrogates with real-time thermal observation \cite{r98}; cloud platforms perform in-situ life prediction at fleet scale \cite{r99}; second-life workflows use impedance-based accelerated SOH estimation to triage retired packs \cite{r100}, and learned battery models with numerical search estimate real-time state-of-power \cite{r101}. Non-electrical sensing broadens the measurement base --- ultrasonic signatures provide a SOC channel orthogonal to voltage sensing \cite{r102} --- and ML mappings from equivalent-circuit parameters to electrochemical properties give classical BMS models a physically interpretable learned layer \cite{r103}. These settings differ in latency budgets, error tolerance, and update cadence, and the field is beginning to design for those differences. Production-side mapping review \cite{r104}, coating interpretability \cite{r95}, and defect detection \cite{r89} complete the picture: cell quality is itself a BMS input.

\subsection{Foundation models and LLM-based management}

The classified subset's youngest theme is generative, and it arrives with a prior-art caveat: LLM-based battery state estimation already exists beyond this corpus: a 2025 IEEE Transactions on Energy Conversion letter \cite{r29} that this review's Crossref retrieval failed to return --- a recall miss, not a venue exclusion --- so no study in this corpus can claim to be the first of its kind. Within the subset the published studies are of two distinct kinds: a domain-specific LLM for risk analysis of battery \textit{energy-storage-system} incidents --- station-side rather than onboard technology --- \cite{r14}, and HA-LLM, which crosses the estimation boundary by using field vehicle data to estimate SOH with uncertainty-aware control, coordinating vehicle and cloud \cite{r15}. The honest framing of \cite{r15} matters for everything that follows: LLM-based estimation is no longer hypothetical --- what remains normative is the claim that before any LLM-based estimator feeds actuation, it must publish calibrated intervals, structured outputs, and a latency budget, and it must not hold direct cell-level protection authority; that role belongs to physics-based envelopes and calibrated numeric estimators. \section{Research Gaps, Reporting Standards, and Future Directions}

Cross-referencing the bibliometric analysis of Section III with the function-level reviews yields six structural gaps. (1) Validation practice: 17 of the 88 primary papers use within-cell or unsupervised k-fold validation, and only 8 of the 88 primary papers (every online demonstration in the subset is a primary application) evaluate online or on fleets; standardized cross-cell, cross-chemistry, and lab-to-fleet transfer protocols should become reporting norms, as already practiced by the transfer-learning and general-framework studies \cite{r69}\cite{r68}. (2) Two-pole data fragmentation: the three public benchmarks sustain 23 primary papers and 36 rest on private laboratory cells that are rarely released; federated learning over fleets, anonymized fleet-data releases, and multi-institution cycling campaigns --- in the spirit of the 179-cell pipeline \cite{r49} --- would diversify the evidence base, and papers should at minimum disclose split definitions, seeds, and compute budgets. (3) Edge deployment: only isolated works treat onboard compute, memory, and latency as first-class constraints \cite{r59}\cite{r105}; quantization-aware training, resource-constrained architecture search, and cloud--edge hybrid designs are the natural research program. (4) Trust: uncertainty must be calibrated rather than post-hoc \cite{r77}, interpretability must bound behavior rather than merely attribute it, and hybrid physics--data architectures are the most credible certification path because part of their behavior is analytically specified. (5) Foundation-model integration: an LLM-based estimator \cite{r15} and the risk-analysis LLM \cite{r14} suggest a division of labor --- foundation models for reasoning and orchestration, conventional ML for calibrated estimation, physics models for safety envelopes --- that should be formalized with grounding and latency guarantees. (6) Under-served functions: balancing is nearly untouched beyond pack-level insight \cite{r30}, charging needs certifiable adaptive protocols, and thermal management remains simulation-dominated \cite{r25}; these are control problems where errors act on the battery, so closing them requires the safety-case machinery of gap 4 as much as new algorithms. Two follow-on subsections turn these gaps outward --- first to what the automotive safety standards actually demand of these functions, then to how the emerging 2027--2030 research directions map onto them.

\subsection{From corpus gaps to safety-standard requirements}

The standards invoked in Section I --- UN GTR No. 20 (electric-vehicle safety, established in the Global Registry in 2018 \cite{r106}; Amendment 1, submitted by the European Commission on behalf of the Informal Working Group on Electric Vehicle Safety as formal document ECE/TRANS/WP.29/GRSP/2026/4 (status report ECE/TRANS/WP.29/GRSP/2026/3) \cite{r107}, considered at the 79th GRSP session in June 2026 and, at this review's 18 October 2026 finalization, pending before WP.29 with adoption listed for its 17--20 November 2026 session review's retrieval date, adds thermal-propagation provisions with two compliance options: a physical propagation test or a documented risk-management approach ), GB 38031 (GB 38031-2020, now superseded, and the strengthened GB 38031-2025 \cite{r108}, effective July 2026, which replaces the fire/explosion-warning criterion with no-fire/no-explosion thermal-diffusion requirements and adds bottom-impact and fast-charge-cycle tests), ISO 26262:2018 (functional safety of road-vehicle E/E systems \cite{r109}), ISO/PAS 8800:2024 (safety and artificial intelligence --- the road-vehicle extension of ISO 26262 and ISO 21448 to learned components \cite{r110}), and ISO 21448 (SOTIF \cite{r111}) --- do not prescribe estimation algorithms, but they define what a deployed estimator or controller must be able to evidence, and Table VI translates the subset's gaps into those terms. The mapping is this review's synthesis at requirement level rather than clause level, and it is stated so that it can be checked against the standards texts themselves: the point is not legal advice but that a validation protocol reported in a paper corresponds, or does not, to evidence a safety case can use. The inference rule behind every ``supports a standards argument'' statement in this section is explicit: a paper's evidence can support such an argument only if it demonstrates performance on at least one held-out integration unit (a cross-cell holdout or stronger isolation protocol) and reports the environment in which that evidence was obtained; by construction, within-cell and k-fold evidence cannot support the argument, because the safety case is about unseen cells and unseen duty. Three consequences stand out for automotive deployment. Within-cell and k-fold validation (17 primary papers) cannot support an ISO 26262 argument, because the safety case is about unseen cells and unseen duty and the protocol never tests an unseen unit; a standards argument must therefore be \textit{argued} from the isolation evidence, not \textit{asserted} from headline accuracy. Thermal-propagation early-warning latency is a timing requirement, so fault-detection papers evaluated on curated laboratory windows under-report both the false-alarm rate and the detection-delay distribution the GB 38031-2025 / GTR-20 escape window needs. And charging or thermal control strategies validated only in simulation lack the hardware-in-the-loop evidence base an automotive release process expects. The reporting checklist below (Table VIII) is deliberately aligned with this mapping --- reporting an isolation argument and a validation environment is the minimum on which any standards conversation can start.

\subsection{Position relative to prior reviews}

The closest in scope among recent reviews is the performance-centric review of Prasanna et al. \cite{r27}, which examines 240 peer-reviewed publications (from 361 initially retrieved records) spanning the whole EV powertrain --- battery management alongside charging systems, motor control, and energy-consumption prediction --- and aggregates reported performance metrics (RMSE, R²) into a quantitative benchmarking of algorithm families, concluding that hybrid deep-learning architectures (LSTM-DDQN, CNN-BiGRU, transformer-based frameworks) consistently achieve superior accuracy (R² > 0.95, RMSE < 0.05). This review differs from that work in scope, unit of analysis, and question asked. In scope, it is narrower and deeper --- battery-management functions only --- which permits function-level cuts such as the standards mapping of Section IX-A and the per-function validation audit. In unit of analysis, it is protocol-centric rather than performance-centric: instead of pooling reported accuracy figures, it codes how each study's validation protocol isolates (or fails to isolate) its generalization claim. The two approaches are complementary, and the complementarity is pointed: an aggregation of self-reported RMSE and R² may inherit, unmeasured, the validation weaknesses this review quantifies within its own subset --- there, 17 of 88 primary papers validate on information-leaking splits, and headline accuracies concentrate on three shared benchmarks --- so where Prasanna et al. answer \textit{which algorithm family performs best as reported}, this review asks \textit{how much the reports can be trusted, and what a study must disclose for its claims to be checkable}. Adjacent cross-functional surveys exist for degradation and estimation methods; Table VII states the comparison axis by axis across all of them: a two-tier corpus with record-level screening provenance, a within-corpus measurement of validation protocol strength across functions, a standards-grounded gap analysis, and a released, re-run-able analysis pipeline. Where prior reviews ask ``which method works best for function X,'' this review asks ``which claims can actually be checked --- and what would it cost the reader to trust them,'' which is the question a scoping review with a curated classified subset is positioned to answer, and the question that performance-centric aggregation cannot ask of itself.

\subsection{Outlook: what the next revision cycle of this literature should produce}

Concretely, and tied to the gaps above, the highest-value near-term work falls in six directions. (a) \textit{Duty-cycle-aware validation}: public cross-cell benchmarks that pair cycling data with recorded EV drive profiles, so that cross-chemistry and cross-domain generalization can be tested against transportation duty rather than laboratory schedules; (b) \textit{federated fleet evidence}: privacy-preserving SOH/SOC studies over live fleets ($\approx$2\% of the eligible corpus today), with the 179-cell pipeline as the template; (c) \textit{benchmark diversification}: multi-institution cycling campaigns two orders of magnitude larger than MATR/NASA/CALCE and released with DOI-backed artifacts, addressing the two-pole fragmentation of Section III-C; (d) \textit{pack- and vehicle-level ML}: estimation validated at the integration level where UN GTR 20 and GB 38031 requirements bite, instead of cell-level evidence transplanted upward; (e) \textit{functionally safe learning}: ML estimators whose uncertainty handling and assurance arguments are specified against ISO 26262 safety goals and ISO/PAS 8800, with ISO 21448/SOTIF reasoning for capability limitations, making certifiable charging and thermal control reachable; and (f) \textit{honest foundation-model integration}: retrieval-grounded LLM agents with published latency and calibration budgets, capable of the coordination role while estimation authority stays with calibrated numeric models --- extending, not contradicting, the corpus's LLM estimator \cite{r15}. A guardrail on all six directions: the BMS-ML-RC checklist below is designed to be attached to a paper at submission, so the next revision cycle of this literature arrives auditable by construction.

\subsection{A reporting checklist for machine-learning BMS studies}

The most concrete artifact this review can offer is procedural. Modeled on the CONSORT and PRISMA checklists, Table VIII proposes a twelve-item BMS-ML reporting checklist (BMS-ML-RC): the minimum metadata a study should disclose for its accuracy claims to be comparable, reproducible, and poolable --- and, per Section IX-A, admissible in a safety discussion. Its derivation is explicit: items 4, 5, and 11 operationalize the validation-protocol classes defined in Section II-C and measured in the classified subset; items 1--3 and 6--10 respond to the reporting omissions observed across Sections III--VIII; item 12 is the artifact-availability norm this paper itself follows. What the checklist is and is not: it is a proposal grounded in the classified subset's measured weaknesses, not a community-validated instrument --- no Delphi or expert-consensus process was run for it, only three items are score-measured in this corpus, and it has not been applied as an audit to all 103 studies. The review's own reporting follows the PRISMA-ScR flow structure (Fig. 2), but the protocol was not prospectively registered; where this section says a practice \textit{should} be reported, that is a recommendation supported by the evidence above, and we suggest journals consider it as a submission checklist.

\section{Conclusion}

This review has examined machine learning in battery management on a two-tier evidence base: a rule-based eligible corpus of 799 journal articles from which field-level shares are measured within a documented capped retrieval, and an in-depth classified subset of 103 studies through which method, data, and validation practice are examined with a 30-paper blind reliability check and label-noise sensitivity ranges. Three conclusions stand out. First, and most critically, the proliferation of published accuracy claims is not matched by validation progress: only 8 of the 88 primary papers (8 of the 103 classified studies) demonstrate online or fleet operation, 17 of 88 primary papers use isolation-weakened splits, and 23 rest on three public benchmarks while 36 rest on private laboratory cells; the physics-informed validation contrast is directionally consistent under both reported definitions, statistically unestablished and underpowered (protocol-only 36\% versus 52\%, p = 0.32; including-hardware 59\% versus 74\%, p = 0.19), so it is reported as no detectable difference, with meaningful differences in either direction not excluded --- an open hypothesis rather than a settled result. Second, the center of gravity is estimation at cell level: measured as shares of the eligible corpus, the SOC--SOH--prognostics union tags 366 of 799 records (46\% --- about half), whereas vehicle- and pack-level practice remains thin --- precisely the level at which UN GTR 20, GB 38031, and ISO 26262 pose their requirements. Third, within the classified subset, method families have shifted from classical feature-based learners to deep sequence models and now physics-informed hybrids, with LLM-based studies appearing from 2025--2026 --- though LLM-based battery estimation began in other venues; this is a within-subset sequence that the eligible pool's method-theme tags do not mirror, so no field-level family rise is measurable at either tier. The near-term agenda follows directly: standardized cross-domain validation, diversified fleet data, pack- and vehicle-level ML aligned with the standards mapping of Section IX, calibrated uncertainty, and a strict division of labor between foundation models, calibrated estimators, and physics-based safety envelopes. If the last five years were about discovering what machine learning can estimate about a battery, the next five will be decided by what it can be trusted to do --- onboard, at fleet scale, across chemistries, and over full cell lifetimes.
